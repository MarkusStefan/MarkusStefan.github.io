MedGraph · Team project · 2025 · Unpublished

Learning patient representations from clinical graphs

Turn incomplete clinical records into relationships, learn their structure, and use it to assess cardiac amyloidosis.

Ante Milicevic · Nemanja Saveski · Markus Köfler
Knowledge Graphs course project, with clinical input from Medical University of Vienna.

01 / Records → relations

Give clinical observations a shared vocabulary

Demographics, blood tests, medical history, and cardiac measurements arrive as a sparse table. We translated observed values into triples: patient → relation → encoded marker value. For example, age 64 becomes patient → has_age → age_60_70.

Encode clinical meaning

Domain-informed bins turn continuous measurements into categories. Marker values link to their marker and clinical category, so individual observations sit within a common schema.

Keep useful observations

We filtered sparse or unsuitable variables, removed referral clues that reveal the target, and masked MRI measurements taken after scintigraphy. An unrecorded marker creates no observation edge.

02 / Relations → representations

Learn vectors from the graph

Using PyKEEN, we trained models including TransE and DistMult to score observed triples above corrupted alternatives. Each patient, marker value, and relation receives a learned vector: a compact representation of its role in the graph.

  1. EncodeClinical records become typed relationships between entities.
  2. EmbedKG models learn entity and relation vectors from those triples.
  3. Inspect & predictExplore patient embeddings, then use the vectors as GNN features.

The opening figure shows DistMult’s patient vectors. We used these projections to inspect representation structure, alongside link prediction to assess how well models recover held-out outcome links.

03 / Representations → prediction

Combine clinical evidence through message passing

We used KG vectors as node and edge features in a heterogeneous graph, with clinical categories and marker-specific relations. GNNs, including GraphSAGE, aggregate information from connected nodes to learn representations tailored to a binary target: scintigraphy Perugini grade ≥ 2 versus grades 0–1.

The screening trade-off: the report evaluated GraphSAGE across earlier and later patient cohorts. Favoring recall reduced missed positive cases, but produced many false positives.

Earlier screening motivated the project. The retrospective experiments do not establish prediction of future disease or clinical readiness. Further work includes validating bin choices, checking information leakage across training stages, and comparing against strong tabular baselines.
